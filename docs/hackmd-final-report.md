# Stage 1 — Characterizing the Baseline

The original `solver.c` solves the 2×2×2 cube by constructing a complete breadth-first search table over all

$$
7! \times 3^6
= 5040 \times 729
= 3,674,160
$$

legal states.

For my later implementation, I retained `solver.c` as a host-side correctness oracle rather than using its complete BFS table as the target solver.

## Baseline Representation

A cube state can be factored into corner permutation and corner orientation.

The implementation fixes one corner and stores the remaining seven movable corners:

```c
typedef struct {
    uint8_t p[7];
    uint8_t o[7];
} state_t;
```

`p[i]` records which cubie occupies position `i`.

`o[i]` records its orientation and has the value 0, 1, or 2.

The permutation component has

$$
7! = 5040
$$

possible configurations.

Only six orientation values are independent because the final orientation is determined by the orientation-sum constraint. Therefore the orientation component contains

$$
3^6 = 729
$$

states.

Together they produce

$$
5040 \times 729 = 3,674,160
$$

legal cube states.

## Mathematical Foundations

### The Group Generated by R, B, and D

Fix the position and orientation of the upper-front-left corner. The right, back, and down face turns, denoted by $R$, $B$, and $D$, leave this corner unchanged. Their compositions generate the fixed-corner cube group:

$$
G=\langle R,B,D\rangle.
$$

The group operation is composition of moves. The identity corresponds to doing nothing, and composition is associative. Every move has an inverse: a clockwise quarter turn can be undone by a counterclockwise quarter turn. In particular,

$$
R^4=B^4=D^4=e,
\qquad
R^{-1}=R^3,
$$

with analogous identities for $B$ and $D$. The group is noncommutative: changing the order of moves can change the resulting state.

### Corner Orientation Invariant

Represent each corner orientation by an element of $\{0,1,2\}$, corresponding to no twist, a positive twist, or a negative twist modulo 3. Using a consistent corner-orientation convention, legal face turns preserve the total orientation modulo 3.

A face turn permutes the corners and changes their orientations by increments whose sum is zero modulo 3. Since the solved cube has total orientation zero, every reachable state satisfies

$$
\sum_{i=0}^{7} o_i \equiv 0 \pmod 3.
$$

The fixed corner has $o_0=0$, so the seven movable corners satisfy

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{7} o_i \equiv 0 \pmod 3.
$$

Consequently, only six of their orientations are independent. The seventh is determined by

$$
o_7 \equiv -\sum_{i=1}^{6}o_i \pmod 3.
$$

### State-Space Size

After fixing one corner, the remaining seven distinguishable corners have $7!$ possible permutations. Their six independent orientations have $3^6$ possible assignments. Therefore, the number of legal states is

$$
|G|=7!\times 3^6
=5040\times729
=3,674,160.
$$

There is no additional restriction requiring the corner permutation to be even: a quarter turn cycles four corners and is itself an odd permutation. The orientation constraint must still hold.

The counting argument enumerates the candidate states satisfying these constraints. Exhaustively reaching all 3,674,160 distinct states using $R$, $B$, and $D$ verifies that these generators cover the entire represented state space.

### Generators and the Half-Turn Metric

Although $G$ is generated by the three quarter turns $R$, $B$, and $D$, the search uses the following nine moves:

$$
S=\{R,R^{-1},R^2,\;
B,B^{-1},B^2,\;
D,D^{-1},D^2\}.
$$

Under the half-turn metric (HTM), each listed move costs one step. Thus, a 180-degree turn costs one step, just like a clockwise or counterclockwise 90-degree turn.

### The Cayley Graph

The search space can be represented by the Cayley graph $\operatorname{Cay}(G,S)$. Each vertex represents a group element, identified with the cube state obtained by applying that element to the solved cube. An edge connects $g$ to $gs$ for each $s\in S$.

Because $S$ contains the inverse of every move, each edge can be traversed in either direction. Every edge has unit cost. Solving a cube therefore means finding a shortest path from its vertex to the identity vertex $e$, which represents the solved state.

### Why Exhaustive BFS Establishes Diameter 11

Breadth-first search starts at $e$ and explores vertices in increasing distance order. Because every edge costs one step, the first discovery of a state determines its shortest distance from $e$.

To establish a maximum distance of 11, an exhaustive BFS must verify that:

1. All 3,674,160 legal states are reached.
2. Every reached state has distance at most 11.
3. At least one state has distance exactly 11.

The second condition proves an upper bound of 11, while the third proves that a smaller bound is impossible.

This initially establishes the greatest distance from the solved state. To obtain the diameter—the greatest distance between any two vertices—we also use the symmetry of the Cayley graph. Left multiplication preserves edges and distances, so for any $g,h\in G$,

$$
d(g,h)=d(e,g^{-1}h).
$$

Thus, every distance between two states is also a distance from the identity to some state. An exhaustive BFS with maximum distance 11 therefore establishes

$$
\operatorname{diam}(\operatorname{Cay}(G,S))=11.
$$

### Connection to the Baseline Solver

When BFS first discovers a state $v$ from a state $u$ using move $s$, it can store $s^{-1}$ at $v$. Applying this stored move returns to $u$, whose distance from the solved state is one smaller.

Following these stored moves repeatedly therefore reaches the solved state along a shortest path. This explains why the baseline can store only one move toward the solution per state, while still requiring preprocessing and storage across the entire state space.

## Baseline Target Cost

The baseline performs a breadth-first search from the solved state and stores one move toward the solution for every legal state. This requires exploring the entire state space before solving an input, resulting in substantial preprocessing work and memory usage. Repeated conversions between cube states and their integer indices also contribute to the computational cost.

The original full BFS approach requires a queue and a value for every complete cube state. This is unsuitable for the 128 KiB target-data budget and expensive to simulate in Ripes.

# Stage 2 — Representation and Search Algorithm

## Iterative IDA* Search

I replaced the complete target-side BFS search with an iterative IDA* search.

The search does not use recursion or heap allocation. Instead, the active path is maintained explicitly using:

```text
states[12]
moves[12]
depth
limit
```

`states[12]` stores the cube state at each search depth.

`moves[12]` records which move is currently being explored at each depth.

`depth` is the current depth.

`limit` is the current IDA* bound.

The first bound is initialized from:

```text
heuristic(start)
```

and the bound is increased iteratively until 11.

For every candidate state:

```text
new_depth = depth + 1
```

and the branch is rejected when

```text
new_depth + heuristic(candidate) > limit
```

This preserves the low-memory behavior of depth-first search while using a heuristic to avoid exploring branches that cannot satisfy the current bound.

### Search-tree pruning

Consecutive moves on the same face are not explored.

For example:

```text
R R  = R2
R R' = identity
```

Therefore an optimal HTM solution never needs two consecutive moves on the same face.

The root has nine possible moves. After the first move, at most six moves remain at every depth.

---

## First Heuristic: Incorrect Cubies

The first heuristic counted a cubie as incorrect when either its permutation or its orientation differed from the solved state.

A move affects at most four corners, so the initial lower bound was:

```c
h = (incorrect + 3) / 4;
```

equivalent to

$$
h=\lceil incorrect/4\rceil.
$$

This heuristic was inexpensive but weak because only seven movable cubies are represented.

---

## Permutation and Orientation Pattern Databases

I then separated the state abstraction into permutation and orientation.

The first implementation used two distance arrays:

```text
perm_dist[5040]
orient_dist[729]
```

Their total storage was:

$$
5040+729=5769\text{ bytes}.
$$

`perm_dist` contains shortest distances when only permutation is considered.

`orient_dist` contains shortest distances when only orientation is considered.

The initial target implementation constructed these tables by repeatedly propagating distances from the solved abstract state.

For each abstract state it:

1. reconstructed a representative state,
2. applied each of the nine moves,
3. calculated the next abstract index,
4. attempted to update that entry with the current distance plus one,
5. repeated until no shorter value was produced.

The search heuristic became:

```text
max(incorrect_lower_bound,
    permutation_distance,
    orientation_distance)
```

## State Representation and Search Correctness

### Choice of State Representation

The solver fixes the position and orientation of the upper-front-left corner and stores the remaining seven corners in two byte arrays, p[7] and o[7]. The first records which cubie occupies each position; the second records its orientation modulo 3. The state payload is 14 bytes.

Although the seventh permutation and orientation entries can be reconstructed from the others, storing them simplifies direct move application. Retaining full states avoids decoding ranks before every move. Heuristic lookup uses the first six entries directly, trading a larger sparse permutation table for simpler indexing.

The iterative search stores 12 states for depths 0 through 11, requiring 168 bytes of state payload. Move counters and cached rejection masks are additional storage. Ancestor states remain available during backtracking, so no inverse move is needed to restore them. The search requires neither recursion nor heap allocation.

### Termination

For any fixed limit, the search tree is finite: each node has at most nine candidates and the depth cannot exceed the limit. Each depth retains its last attempted move, so backtracking continues with the next candidate rather than restarting the node.

The outer loop increases the limit by one and stops after 11. The algorithm therefore terminates even if no solution is found. For valid states, the established HTM diameter of 11 guarantees a solution within this bound, provided pruning preserves an optimal path.

### Admissibility of the Pattern-Database Heuristic

Let $d(s)$ denote the shortest complete solution length. Let $h_p(s)$ and $h_o(s)$ be exact distances in the permutation-only and orientation-only abstractions. Every complete solution also solves both abstractions. Projecting its moves gives an abstract path of the same length, so

$$
h_p(s)\le d(s),\qquad h_o(s)\le d(s).
$$

Therefore,

$$
h_0(s)=\max(h_p(s),h_o(s))\le d(s).
$$

Taking the maximum preserves admissibility. Adding the distances is not generally safe, because one move can improve both permutation and orientation.

### Optimality of Iterative Deepening

The initial limit is an admissible lower bound. At depth $g$, a candidate is rejected when $g+h(s)>L$, where $L$ is the current limit. Because $h(s)\le d(s)$, such a candidate cannot finish within the remaining budget $L-g$.

The canonical pruning described below preserves the lexicographically first shortest solution. On this path, the admissible bound never exceeds the remaining solution length. When the limit reaches the optimal length, this path therefore survives both heuristic and sequence pruning. Earlier limits cannot contain a shorter solution. The first returned solution is consequently optimal in HTM.

## Additional Search Pruning

### Inverse-Orientation Lower Bound

A cube transformation and its inverse have equal distance from the identity. Reversing a move sequence and replacing each move by its inverse gives a sequence for the inverse transformation with the same length. Since all inverse moves belong to the move set,

$$
d(s^{-1})=d(s).
$$

The orientation abstraction of the inverse cube therefore supplies another admissible bound:

$$
h_i(s)=h_o(s^{-1})\le d(s^{-1})=d(s).
$$

The final heuristic is

$$
h(s)=\max\bigl(h_p(s),h_o(s),h_o(s^{-1})\bigr).
$$

For the position-to-cubie convention, inverse orientation is constructed by

$$
o_{\mathrm{inv}}[p[i]]=(-o[i])\bmod 3.
$$

This both relocates and negates the orientation; negating each entry in its original position is insufficient. In the implementation, zero remains zero and nonzero orientation is replaced by 3 minus its value. The first six inverse-orientation entries index the existing orientation distance table, so no additional pattern database is needed.

The inverse lookup is performed only when the original maximum of the permutation and orientation bounds fits the remaining budget. If the original bound already rejects the candidate, skipping the inverse lookup saves work without changing the decision.

### Canonical Move-Suffix Pruning

The table generator enumerates move sequences through length five, ordered first by length and then by move order. For each cube transformation, the first sequence is its canonical representative. Another sequence is rejected if it has a shorter or equal-length lexicographically earlier equivalent.

Consecutive same-face moves are rejected directly because they combine into one HTM move or cancel. Additional tables cover noncanonical sequences of lengths three, four, and five. These are equivalences of transformations, so they hold from any parent state, not just the solved state.

### Why Canonical Pruning Preserves an Optimal Solution

Consider the lexicographically first shortest solution. If it contained a rejected substring with a shorter equivalent, replacement would produce a shorter solution, contradicting optimality. If the equivalent had equal length but appeared earlier, replacement would produce an earlier solution of the same length, contradicting the lexicographic choice.

Thus this optimal solution has no forbidden substring and survives. Preserving every optimal path is unnecessary; at least one survives the combined rules.

### Table Layout and Cached Masks

The three rejection tables occupy

$$
9^2+9^3+9^4=81+729+6561=7371\text{ bytes}.
$$

Each byte encodes the six possible continuations on faces different from the preceding face. The helper expands these bits into a nine-move mask and adds the three same-face rejection bits.

Only the last four ancestor moves are needed to test a new suffix of at most five moves. The mask is computed once on entering a node and cached while its candidates are tried. It is checked before move application and heuristic lookup, avoiding both operations for rejected sequences. Candidate states are written directly into the next-depth slot without modifying active ancestors.

# Stage 3 — Improving the C Implementation

The next objective was to reduce the work that would later appear as RV32I instructions.

## Removing Expensive Integer Operations

Compiling an early RV32I core exposed calls such as:

```text
__mulsi3
__divsi3
__modsi3
__udivsi3
__umodsi3
memcpy
memset
```

Arithmetic was rewritten using operations directly available in RV32I.

Modulo 3 in bounded orientation arithmetic was implemented with comparisons and subtraction.

Move decoding using `/3` and `%3` was replaced by range checks and subtraction.

Constant multiplication was rewritten using shifts and additions or subtractions.

For orientation indexing:

```text
rank * 3
```

became:

```text
(rank << 1) + rank
```

Permutation multipliers from 1 through 7 were similarly decomposed into shift/add/sub sequences.

All 5,040 permutation indices and all 729 orientation indices were checked against their previous results.

| heuristic | 6.10% |

The largest cost came from repeated copying of the 14-byte `state_t`.

In particular, the by-value form of the move implementation caused state structures to be copied across parameters, return values, and assignments.

## solver4 — Pointer-Based Moves and Direct Rotations

The move interface was changed to:

```c
apply_move(
    const state_t *src,
    state_t *dst,
    uint8_t face,
    uint8_t turns
);
```

This avoided passing and returning complete cube states by value.

The implementation also stopped constructing 180° and 270° moves by repeatedly performing quarter turns.

Instead, direct mappings were prepared for all nine HTM moves:

```text
R   R2   R'
B   B2   B'
D   D2   D'
```

Each move directly reads the required source cubies, adjusts their orientations, and writes the destination state.

The resulting ELF no longer contained calls to `memcpy`.

For the same distance-11 input:

```text
solver3 : 435,132,487
solver4 : 184,068,852
```

This reduced the retired-instruction count by approximately 57.7%.

### Why I selected this optimization

The retained profiling record identifies repeated copying of the 14-byte state as the dominant cost in solver3. This made the move interface a useful first target: passing source and destination pointers removes parameter and return-value copies from a routine called throughout the search. Direct mappings for all nine HTM moves also avoid repeating quarter-turn work for half and inverse turns. These changes preserve the search space and heuristic, so they address per-node execution cost without changing which solutions are admissible.

For the same recorded distance-11 input, the combined change reduced retired instructions from 435,132,487 to 184,068,852, a 57.7% reduction, and the resulting ELF contained no memcpy calls. This comparison measures the pointer interface and direct mappings together; it does not isolate their individual contributions. Afterward, permutation indexing accounted for 45.51% of the recorded solver4 profile, motivating the next lookup/indexing changes rather than further work on copying.

# Stage 4 — RV32I Assembly Optimization

## Direct Expansion of the Nine Moves

The search-hot move loop was replaced by nine specialized RV32I paths.

For a specific move, the source cubie positions and orientation changes are constants. Therefore each block directly performs the required loads, orientation adjustment, and stores.

This removed the hot-path costs of:

```text
move_source[] lookups
move_twist[] lookups
the seven-cubie loop branch
dynamic source-index calculation
```

Measured result:

```text
solver7                  53,779,947
direct-move assembly     40,549,573
```

This was the first measured version of the specified test vector below 50 million retired instructions.

### Assembly reasoning

Each of the nine moves has a fixed cubie permutation and fixed orientation adjustments. Explicit assembly paths make those choices constants, avoiding the generic loop's repeated index calculation, move-table selection and loop-control instructions. This matters on RV32I, where general multiplication and division are unavailable and address-generation work must be expressed using simpler instructions.

The retained specified-input comparison falls from 53,779,947 instructions for solver7 to 40,549,573 for the direct-move assembly, approximately 24.6% fewer instructions. That supports the change for this measured input; it is not an exhaustive worst-case result for the historical version. The final assembly separately passed all 2,644 distance-11 inputs, with maximum 38,543,830 instructions.

Specialization trades code bytes for less runtime work. The final assembly has 4,296 bytes of text versus 2,752 bytes for the final GCC -O2 C build, an increase of 1,544 bytes (56.1%). This is a final-version comparison containing several optimizations, not an isolated measurement of move expansion. Its renderer-off static data is 126,079 bytes, within 128 KiB, so the larger code remains compatible with the stated static-data budget.

## Horner-Form Heuristic Indexing

The sparse permutation-table offset was originally equivalent to:

$$
p_0 7^5+p_1 7^4+p_2 7^3+p_3 7^2+p_4 7+p_5.
$$

It was rewritten in Horner form:

$$
(((((p_0\times7+p_1)\times7+p_2)\times7+p_3)\times7+p_4)\times7+p_5).
$$

The orientation index was handled similarly with radix 3.

On RV32I:

```text
x * 7 = (x << 3) - x
x * 3 = (x << 1) + x
```

The permutation index path fell from 37 to 21 instructions, while the orientation path fell from 29 to 21.

Thus one hot heuristic evaluation removed 24 instructions.

Measured result:

```text
direct moves : 40,549,573
Horner       : 34,934,509
```

The difference was:

```text
5,615,064 retired instructions
```

The measurements show that optimizing one dominant operation exposes another. Removing state copies shifted attention to ranking and move generation; host-generated tables and direct sparse lookup then reduced target-side indexing work. Removing a heuristic component and specializing assembly further reduced work per visited node, while the final search pruning reduced how often move generation and heuristic evaluation were needed. A lower cost per node and fewer explored nodes are distinct mechanisms and should not be conflated.

The selected design spends static space on precomputed distance tables and code space on fixed move paths. In exchange, it avoids building the full-state BFS table on the target and avoids expensive dense ranking in the hot heuristic path. The final renderer-off assembly uses 126,079 static bytes, leaving 4,993 bytes below the 131,072-byte limit, plus a separately reported 4,096-byte reserved stack. On the specified input it executes 15,147,934 instructions versus 31,343,837 for the final C build, approximately 51.7% fewer. The all-distance-11 maximum of 38,543,830 leaves 11,456,170 instructions below the 50-million limit. These claims follow the recorded build and measurement conventions; they do not establish simulator wall-time speedups on every host.

# Development Process Reflection

The retained artifacts show three recurring difficulties: reducing target instruction cost without exceeding static memory, preserving optimal solutions while changing the search, and separating simulated execution checks from actual GUI observations. The progression from by-value states to pointer moves, then to precomputed lookup structures and specialized assembly, follows the measured cost shifts described above. The final C and assembly also have different performance results, so the report identifies the exact implementation for each number instead of treating all solver7 files as interchangeable.

Correctness evidence was kept separate from performance evidence. Host checks cover heuristic admissibility and optimal C solutions, while the assembly distance-11 sweep replays each returned path independently. CLI model passes and RAM renderer checks were useful but did not establish visible LED or pipeline behavior. That gap was closed by actual five-stage observations and twelve consecutive LED breakpoint captures, with every captured frame checked against the expected facelets.

The GUI work also exposed a practical verification problem: an attempted click or stale screenshot does not prove that a program loaded or a frame advanced. Recording the breakpoint, checking stopped execution and comparing complete frames made the final observation auditable. # Development Summary

This retrospective summarizes retained source versions and recorded AI-assisted measurements; it was compiled after development rather than recorded as three contemporaneous HackMD revisions.

| Phase | Main changes and rationale | Representative result |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Search baseline — solver2–3** | Replaced full-state preprocessing with iterative IDA* and admissible permutation/orientation bounds; added a freestanding RV32I runtime and solution replay. | solver3: **435,132,487** instructions. Profiling identified state copying, move execution and indexing as major costs. |
| **2. C implementation — solver4–7** | Used pointer-based moves and precomposed rotations, then precomputed transitions and direct heuristic lookup. Removed the rarely stronger incorrect-cubie bound to reduce hot-path work. | solver4 → solver5 → solver6 → solver7: **184,068,852 → 110,209,922 → 69,539,968 → 53,779,947** instructions, trading additional table space for less computation. |
| **3. Search pruning and assembly** | solver7_search adds the inverse-orientation bound, canonical move-suffix pruning, cached masks and direct child-state writes. The assembly separately adds specialized moves, Horner indexing, early permutation rejection and redundant-work removal, then incorporates the search improvements. | New compiled C: **31,343,837**; final assembly: **15,147,934** instructions. Final assembly static data: **126,079 bytes**. |

Counts use input 21345671111111 on RV32_ISS, without extensions or rendering, including startup, initialization where applicable, search, replay, output and exit. These combined changes are not isolated ablations. The final assembly's separate sweep over all 2,644 distance-11 inputs has a maximum of **38,543,830** instructions; exhaustive C verification and target assembly testing have different scopes.
